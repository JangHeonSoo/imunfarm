By late October the sun sets before six in Korea. Come back from the field and the yard is already dark. A working entrance sensor light goes unnoticed; one that stays on all night or flickers gets noticed fast. And sheds or farm huts without wiring have no light at all.

> Bottom line: <strong>If an entrance sensor light will not turn off, first look for anything moving or changing temperature in its detection zone and check whether the on-time setting is long.</strong> If that fails, replace the whole fixture: switch off the breaker and move the two supply wires to the new light's terminals. For shed and hut doors without wiring, use battery or USB-rechargeable wireless sensor lights.

## 1. When it will not turn off

Most sensor lights use passive infrared (PIR), which reacts to moving body heat, so any temperature change in range can trigger them.

| Symptom                        | Check first                                                      |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stays on a long time           | On-time setting; each detection restarts the timer               |
| Turns on with nobody there     | Cold draft from the door, heater or boiler exhaust, plants, pets |
| On all night                   | Constant motion in range; dust or webs on the lens               |
| Reacts to neighbors or hallway | Microwave (radar) sensors can see through thin walls or doors    |
| Flickers or will not light     | Worn-out driver or sensor; replace the fixture                   |

Fixtures with dials usually offer TIME, SENS and LUX adjustments. Shorten the time and lower sensitivity one step. Many ceiling entrance lights have no adjustment, and since a round LED sensor light costs around KRW 10,000, replacement is often cheaper than repair.

## 2. Replacing a wired sensor light

1. Switch off the circuit breaker at the panel, not just the wall switch.
2. Remove the cover and unscrew the base.
3. Confirm the wires are dead with a voltage tester.
4. Release the two wires from the old terminals.
5. Push them fully into the new light's terminals so no bare copper shows.
6. Fix the base, fit the cover and restore power.
7. Check that it lights, goes off, and lights again when you wave.

Korea's Electrical Construction Business Act enforcement decree lists attaching wires to the terminals of electrical appliances as minor work that does not require a licensed contractor. New wiring, extra switches or a breaker that keeps tripping should go to an electrician. While at the panel, press the residual-current breaker's test button; Korea Electrical Safety Corporation advises replacing it if power does not cut off.

## 3. Battery and rechargeable wireless lights

Wireless lights stick on with tape or magnets and run on batteries or a USB-charged cell. They suit shed and hut doors, yard steps and utility rooms.

| Type             | Pros                       | Cons                                        |
| ---------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Battery          | No charging, months of use | Ongoing battery cost; drains faster in cold |
| USB rechargeable | No battery cost            | Must remember to charge; cell wears out     |
| Wired            | Brightest, little upkeep   | Only where wiring exists                    |

They are much dimmer than wired lights: think door handle and footpath, not the whole yard. For outdoor areas, a [solar garden light](/en/blog/farm-shed-solar-light-buying-guide/) charged by day is a better fit.

## 4. Shed and farm-hut doors

Check water protection (IP44 for splashes under eaves, IP65 or higher for direct rain), detection range, and cold: batteries drain faster below freezing, so mount wireless units inside the door or under the eaves. A wireless light inside the shed door plus a solar sensor light outside covers most rural needs without new wiring.
